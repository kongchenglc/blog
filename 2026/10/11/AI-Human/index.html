Questions for Exploration: Human Intelligence and AI

Open questions about thought, learning, and the evolution of intelligence, organized with related papers and projects.

1. Where Do New Ideas Come From?

1.1 Spontaneous thoughts, memory, and new input

Q: Why do possible solutions sometimes appear during a shower or a walk?

Related work: Baird et al., Inspired by Distraction (Psychological Science, 2012) studies creative incubation during an undemanding activity. Oppezzo and Schwartz (2014) directly compares walking and sitting on creative-thinking tasks. Sio and Ormerod (Psychological Bulletin, 2009) provides a meta-analysis of incubation effects. These distinguish breaks, mind wandering, and walking; they do not establish a single explanation for everyday inspiration.

Q: How might new input and existing memories contribute to an idea?

Related work: Addis, Wong, and Schacter (Neuropsychologia, 2007) compares neural processes in remembering past events and imagining future ones. Schacter and Addis (Philosophical Transactions B, 2007) reviews constructive memory and future simulation. Both inform the memory side of this question; neither directly explains how new sensory input becomes a creative idea.

1.2 Novelty and idea generation in AI

Q: Can an LLM produce an idea that is genuinely new?

Related work: Boden, Creativity and Artificial Intelligence (Artificial Intelligence, 1998) distinguishes forms of creativity and meanings of novelty. Si et al., Can LLMs Generate Novel Research Ideas? (ICLR 2025) evaluates generated NLP research proposals using expert judgments. The AI Scientist (2024 preprint) implements idea generation and literature-based novelty checks. None establishes that an idea is absent from the model’s training data.

Q: What distinguishes a useful idea from an arbitrary combination, and how could an agent choose what to pursue?

Related work: Runco and Jaeger, The Standard Definition of Creativity (Creativity Research Journal, 2012) discusses originality and effectiveness as criteria. Si et al. (ICLR 2025) examines expert evaluation, idea diversity, and limitations of model self-evaluation. The AI Scientist repository supplies a workflow to inspect, rather than an independently validated standard for choosing research.

1.3 Autonomy, goals, and consciousness

Q: Could an agent initiate work without a new user prompt?

Related work: Wooldridge and Jennings, Intelligent Agents: Theory and Practice (1995) discusses autonomy, reactivity, and proactive behavior. Generative Agents (UIST 2023) implements memory, reflection, and planning in an ongoing simulation. It demonstrates activity without prompting each action, while relying on a configured environment and agent descriptions. GitHub.

Q: Would initiating work or describing its own thoughts imply consciousness?

Related work: Dehaene, Lau, and Kouider (Science, 2017) distinguishes unconscious computation, global availability, and self-monitoring. Butlin et al., Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence (2023 research report/preprint) derives proposed indicators from consciousness theories. These provide frameworks for examining consciousness separately from initiative or self-description; the indicators are not a validated diagnostic test.

2. Can Intelligence Build and Revise Knowledge Through Experience?

Once an agent can act on its own, perhaps through a robot, could it use what it already knows to explore, test new ideas, and revise its understanding? The questions below follow that process, from choosing an investigation to retaining what the experience teaches it.

2.1 Using existing knowledge to guide exploration

Q: How could an agent use existing knowledge to formulate new, testable ideas about its environment?

Related work: King et al., The Automation of Science (Science, 2009) describes Adam, a robot scientist that generated and experimentally tested hypotheses about yeast biology. Boiko et al., Autonomous Chemical Research with Large Language Models (Nature, 2023) connects existing chemical information, language models, and laboratory tools to plan and perform experiments. These offer bounded examples of moving from prior knowledge to investigation, within human-defined research settings.

Q: Without a person specifying every task, how could an agent decide what is worth exploring next?

Related work: Oudeyer, Kaplan, and Hafner (IEEE Transactions on Evolutionary Computation, 2007) studies exploration driven by progress in learning to predict sensorimotor outcomes. Plan2Explore (ICML 2020) uses a learned world model to seek informative experiences before downstream tasks are specified. These implement particular exploration objectives, rather than unrestricted human-like curiosity. Plan2Explore code.

2.2 Testing ideas through interaction with the world

Q: How could a robot choose actions that distinguish between competing explanations, rather than merely complete a task?

Related work: Bongard, Zykov, and Lipson, Resilient Machines Through Continuous Self-Modeling (Science, 2006) chooses physical actions that make competing models of a robot’s body predict different outcomes, then uses observations to refine those models. The Automation of Science (Science, 2009) provides a laboratory counterpart: generating hypotheses and testing them through automated experiments. Both connect action selection to resolving uncertainty in constrained domains.

Q: What would count as new knowledge: a better prediction, a reusable understanding, or a discovery previously unknown to humans?

Related work: Plan2Explore (ICML 2020) learns a predictive model through exploration and evaluates its usefulness for subsequent control tasks. King et al. (Science, 2009) reports biological hypotheses tested by Adam and subsequently checked through manual experiments by the researchers. These provide different evaluation settings for knowledge acquired by an agent and a contribution to scientific knowledge; success in one does not establish the other.

2.3 Correcting earlier beliefs and retaining what is learned

Q: When observations contradict an agent’s expectations, how could it identify and revise the part of its existing understanding that is wrong?

Related work: Bongard et al. (Science, 2006) demonstrates a robot revising its body model after physical damage and generating compensating behavior. Plan2Explore (ICML 2020) updates its predictive world model using observations gathered through exploration. These study model revision through experience; neither provides a general method for distinguishing an incorrect belief from sensor noise, execution failure, or a changed environment.

Q: How could discoveries and corrections become lasting knowledge that remains useful in unfamiliar situations?

Related work: Voyager (TMLR 2024) stores executable skills and evaluates their reuse in new Minecraft worlds and unseen tasks. Plan2Explore (ICML 2020) evaluates whether an exploration-trained world model supports later tasks in simulated environments. These test skill and model reuse, while leaving open how an embodied agent could retain and generalize corrected factual knowledge over long periods. Voyager code.

3. How Can Agents Build Knowledge Together?

If agents can share observations, memories, code, and model weights, how could their separate experiences contribute to collective learning? The questions here concern direct sharing, joint learning, verification, and specialization, without assuming that knowledge must pass through biological-style generations.

3.1 Sharing experience and capabilities

Q: What should agents share: observations, conclusions, reusable skills, or model updates?

Related work: Large Language Models as Tool Makers (ICLR 2024) separates tool creation from tool use and shares reusable programs. McMahan et al., Communication-Efficient Learning of Deep Networks from Decentralized Data (AISTATS 2017) combines locally computed parameter updates into a shared model while keeping training data on clients. These illustrate different things that can be transferred; neither supplies a universal rule for choosing what to share.

Q: Could many agents explore different situations while contributing to one continuously updated model?

Related work: IMPALA (ICML 2018) separates actors collecting experience from learners updating a shared policy, with actors retrieving newer parameters. Federated Averaging (AISTATS 2017) alternates local training and aggregation into a global model. These provide concrete forms of collective learning without generational inheritance, although they differ in where learning happens and how updates are coordinated.

Q: How should the group validate an individual agent’s discovery and correct it if later evidence shows it was wrong?

Related work: Large Language Models as Tool Makers (ICLR 2024) checks generated tools against validation examples before reuse. Multiagent Debate (ICML 2024) studies agents comparing and revising responses, but also reports convergence to confidently shared incorrect answers. These offer examples of testing and critique; neither establishes a complete process for tracing, retracting, and correcting knowledge already propagated through a group.

3.2 Shared models, specialization, and independent judgment

Q: When is one shared model sufficient, and when should agents retain specialized models or local knowledge?

Related work: IMPALA (ICML 2018) trains a shared policy across multiple tasks. Smith et al., Federated Multi-Task Learning (NeurIPS 2017) learns related but distinct models for clients with different data distributions. These provide contrasting approaches to sharing and specialization; their different experimental settings do not establish a general winner.
